KhimiYaar AI

A health information assistant that helps you describe your symptoms, understand your lab results and get a rough fracture screening, all in one conversation. It informs. It never diagnoses.

The name

Khimiyaar is a Khowar word for something that does you good, the way carrots are said to be khimiyaar for your eyes. That is the job of this app.

The problem

People arrive at the doctor unprepared.

People walk into a doctor’s office with vague symptoms and a lab sheet they cannot read. KhimiYaar helps them arrive prepared. It was built with Pakistan in mind, including emergency numbers for the regions, Chitral among them.

The one rule

This is an educational tool. It does not diagnose, prescribe or replace a doctor, and every result is meant to be reviewed by a professional. In an emergency, call emergency services.

The assistant

It tells a personal symptom (“I have a headache”) from a general question (“what is a migraine”), and it does not force an interview onto the second kind.

For the first kind it collects the main symptom, how long it has lasted, your age and your gender. If you skip one, it goes back to it. Then it writes a summary you can hand to a doctor. It picks up English or Roman Urdu from your first message and stays in that language.

The lab analyzer

Upload a photo of a report: blood count, lipids, blood sugar, liver, kidney or thyroid. A vision model reads each test name, value and unit. The values are then checked against reference ranges, and where the medical evidence supports it, the ranges depend on age and sex.

Example: one value, two ranges

A hemoglobin reading is flagged low, normal or high against the range for your sex, not a generic one. If you enter your age and gender later, or just mention them in the chat, the flags update on their own. You never type them twice.

The flagged results flow into the same conversation. So the final summary can say that tiredness and a low hemoglobin reading may be related, phrased as a possibility and never as a diagnosis. Symptoms and lab results are read as one picture, not two separate tools.

Emergency detection

Two layers run on every message.

  1. A fast keyword check that tolerates typos. It looks for body-part and symptom combinations such as chest pain, breathing difficulty or stroke signs.
  2. A second check that reads the recent conversation, because people often reveal an emergency gradually, over several messages.
Example: asking for a number

Ask for “emergency number for Chitral” and you get the national numbers and the regional ones, with no duplicates. The chat is never locked after an alert, so you can keep asking.

The X-ray model

This is a separate model that I trained, not an API call. It is EfficientNetB0, pretrained and then fine-tuned on about 9,000 labelled X-ray images, and it runs on the server’s own CPU. I chose the saved checkpoint by recall, because a missed fracture is worse than a false alarm.

Design decisions

A “no fracture” result is worded carefully

The server writes that sentence, not the frontend. It says the tool can still miss fractures and that you should see a doctor if you have symptoms. A negative result should never read as a clean bill of health.

A pre-check for images that are not X-rays

A simple rule (close to grayscale, with a dark background) stops the model from confidently judging a photo or a document. It is a coarse heuristic, not a trained detector, and I say so in the code.

I did not ship the heatmap

I built a Grad-CAM heatmap to show where the fracture is. The highlights were vague and often in the wrong place. A confident-looking but wrong pointer is worse than no pointer in a health tool, so I removed it. Real localization needs a different kind of model.

Emergency numbers are stored once

National numbers live in one place and are shown next to any regional lookup, never copied into each region.

Where it stands

The MVP is built and tested end to end, locally and inside its Docker container. It is ready to deploy and not hosted yet.
