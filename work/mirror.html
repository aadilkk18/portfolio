The idea

Most games ask you to beat a score. Mirror asks you to be hard to read.

Before every decision, MIRROR secretly commits to a probability for each of two options. Then you pick. The less MIRROR expected your pick, the more points you score. After each round it shows you the exact number it gave your choice, so you can see it learning you.

The decisions are everyday pairs about money, time and effort. “Try a new shop, better price” against “Stay with your usual shop”. “Wait and see when it comes” against “Pay more to get it fast”. There is no right answer on purpose. When one side looks like the good choice, people perform instead of choosing, and that ruins the habits MIRROR is trying to learn.

One round

A session is 8 silent calibration decisions, then 20 live rounds, then a result screen. Each live round has the same beats.

  1. Sealed.The eye is closed. MIRROR has already computed and frozen its probabilities for A and B. Nothing about them is visible.
  2. You choose.You drag a handle toward a side and the eye leans with you. I chose dragging on purpose, so you feel watched while you decide.
  3. Suspense.A short beat of about half a second to nine tenths of one. It is longer when MIRROR is less sure and never depends on the outcome, so the length gives nothing away.
  4. Reveal.The eye opens and looks toward the side it favoured. A number rolls up, like “MIRROR gave your pick 32.77%”. If it is 50% or more the word is Read. Under 50% it is Surprise.
  5. Catch.Rarely, you build a clean pattern and then break it. MIRROR notices and says “On purpose. I noticed.” This does not change your points.

The scoring

If MIRROR gave your pick a probability of p, you score round(100 × ((1 − p)² − 0.25)). It is a shifted Brier score, which is a strictly proper scoring rule. In plain words, the way to do best against an honest predictor is to be genuinely random.

The result screen shows a coin line. It is the exact score a fair coin flipper would have earned against the same sealed guesses, worked out from MIRROR’s own numbers. You win by finishing above it. Within 15 points counts as even. A coin flipper lands above the line about half the time, so the verdict stays honest.

Then comes the receipt. It lists up to three real moments with the exact sealed percentage, like “Decision 14, Surprise, MIRROR gave it 8.41%”. That is how you check that MIRROR really committed before you chose.

Nothing can beat a true coin flip, so the game never says it can. What can keep growing is MIRROR’s ability to catch the ways people fail at being random.

How MIRROR learns

All of it is plain arithmetic in TypeScript on the phone. There is no machine-learning library and no server.

Layer 1: the streak model

An online logistic regression updated after every pick. It starts from a prior I trained once on synthetic data that encodes a published finding: people expect a reversal after a short streak and a continuation after a long one (Rabin and Vayanos, 2010). The prior fades toward the live model as more decisions arrive.

Layer 2: seven experts

The final probability is a weighted mix of seven predictors. One knows nothing and always says 50%. One is the streak model. Four look at what you did after your last 1, 2, 3 and 4 picks. One looks at what you did after the last pick and whether MIRROR had expected it. Each expert is weighted by how well it has done recently. A small share is always spread evenly, so the mix can recover when you change habit. The output is clamped between 5% and 95%.

The prediction is computed once per round and sealed with the context it was made in. The result screen can never disagree with the receipt.

Catching it on purpose

A change-point detector (CUSUM) watches for the signature of a clean streak followed by a sudden reversal. That is evidence of doing it deliberately, not just of MIRROR guessing wrong. It is tuned conservatively, at about a 1% false alarm rate over a session.

The old model is one of the seven experts, so the mixture cannot fall far behind it. Over 31-session careers its worst total loss against the old model was +3.21, and on average it came out 35.06 ahead.

What went wrong first

The first version was a push-your-luck game. You had points at risk, a growing multiplier and a Cash out button. I measured it before I trusted it, and it was broken.

  • Banking decisions mattered about twice as much as outsmarting MIRROR. A good banking rule scored about 148. Never cashing out scored about 76.
  • A farm and strike rhythm scored 312 to 320, far above what real skill earned.
  • Every variant that fixed one problem made the Cash out and Keep going buttons decorative.
  • You could not see what a choice paid before you made it.

So I rebuilt the scoring from one rule: the less MIRROR expected your pick, the more you score. The old version is still in git under the tag pot-economy-v1.

A bug the simulation found

The streak model used raw squared streak lengths. Its weights blew up, and 94 to 97% of predictions claimed near-certainty even for modest habits. After capping and normalising that feature, accuracy for a strong-habit player went from 71.4% to 78.5%. Near-certain claims fell from 93.6% to 0%. Calibration error fell from 0.292 to 0.026.

Android problems, traced before fixed

A stray title bar, a splash image showing through, and a Java version mismatch with Gradle. Java 25 fails with Gradle 8.14.3, so the build needs Java 21. I found the cause of each one before I changed anything.

Rules the design never breaks

  • Offline forever. No network calls. Fonts, music and assets are bundled. It costs nothing to run at any scale.
  • Commit, then reveal. Nothing about MIRROR’s guess is visible before you pick. A whole-screen test checks that the screen is identical whether MIRROR sealed 90% or 30%.
  • Two options, always. Every engine is built around binary decisions.
  • Honest math. No predictor beats true randomness, and the game never implies it can.
  • Every number explained. Exact probabilities appear only at the reveal and in the receipt, never before you pick.

The look follows the same idea. It is silver and brass on near-black, and brass is kept for the one moment MIRROR catches you. Progress and results use one shared set of marks instead of sentences: a dot means read, a ringed dot means you slipped past, a brass diamond means caught. Sounds are synthesised in the browser audio engine with no sample files. A rising cue means you slipped past MIRROR and a falling cue means you were read.

How I built it

The app is React 19, Vite and TypeScript, wrapped for Android with Capacitor. The engines are pure functions in one folder and the screens are thin, so the engines can be tested without a phone.

I built it in step-by-step sessions with Claude. We discussed each design choice, wrote the code and ran the tests. I made the product decisions, ran every build myself and tested on my own phone.

  • Research before decisions. Design and parameter choices were checked against papers and documentation first.
  • Simulation before code. Anything risky was tested in a sandbox first, with a deliberately broken version that the test had to catch.
  • Loophole hunting. I attacked the scoring with 631 strategies: every repeating pattern up to length 8 (510 of them), a grid of 121 two-state habits, and human-style strategies. All were compared against the coin flip.

Limits I know about

  • “No loophole found” means I found none among 631 simulated strategies. It is not a proof, and real people were not part of it.
  • Every learning number above comes from simulated players with idealised habits.
  • Most people have habits, so most will score below the coin line at first. A fair coin flipper earns about +5.5 on a first session, because MIRROR is slightly overconfident on random play. Fixing that is on my list.

What’s next

A playtest with friends. A sentence on the result screen that shows what MIRROR learned about you and is always true. Tuning the coin line. Later, a signed release build.
