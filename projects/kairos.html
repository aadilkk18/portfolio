Kairos

In development

AI decision-intelligence platform

Most of us make big decisions with scattered information and no real structure. Kairos separates what you actually know from what you're only assuming, does the maths properly, and never pretends to be more certain than it is.

The name

Ancient Greek had two words for time. Chronos is the kind you measure: seconds, hours, deadlines. Kairos is the right moment: the window when conditions are actually right to act. Kairos, the product, is a bet on the second one.

The problem

Career switches, big purchases, life calls. People make them with half the facts in their head and no structure, so they either overthink or guess.

The obvious fix is to ask an AI. But if you ask an LLM "which job offer is better?", you get a smooth, confident answer with no calculation behind it. It can't show its working, it can't tell you how fragile the answer is, and it may well have made up the numbers.

The one rule

The LLM narrates. It never calculates. Every number in a Kairos report is computed by a deterministic engine, and the LLM only explains what the engine found.

How it works, with a real example

Take a decision most people face: two job offers. Here is what Kairos does with it, step by step. The numbers below are made up to show the mechanics, but the method is exactly what the engine runs.

  1. You describe the decision in plain language.You type "comparing two job offers". You don't build a model or fill in a template.
  2. Kairos builds the model for you.It creates a domain and works out the objectives that matter: compensation, commute, growth, flexibility, culture.
  3. Each objective is grounded, or honestly rated.If an objective can be tied to real data, Kairos finds it. If it can't, it falls back to a scale you rate yourself.
  4. You review the model.You adjust the objectives, set how much each one matters, add constraints and confirm the data. Nothing runs on assumptions you haven't seen.
  5. Engine A analyses it.Four methods run on the same model: scoring, simulation, sensitivity analysis and a check on how stable your priorities are.
  6. The report explains it.The LLM turns the engine's results into plain language, and every figure it quotes is checked against what was computed.

Step 2 and 3: objectives, and what each one needs

Salary has a currency value. Commute has a time. But "culture" has no universal number, and I refuse to let Kairos invent one just because it's convenient. So each objective is handled according to what it really is:

Example: the objectives for two job offers
ObjectiveKindHow Kairos grounds it
SalaryMeasurable, in moneyA real figure from the offer. Quoted numbers get a small margin of uncertainty.
CommuteMeasurable, in minutesA real estimate. Estimates get a larger margin, because traffic changes.
GrowthNo true numberA 1 to 10 scale that you rate yourself.
FlexibilityNo true numberA 1 to 10 scale that you rate yourself.
CultureNo true numberA 1 to 10 scale that you rate yourself, and Kairos says plainly that it's a judgement.

Step 5a: scoring the options (MCDA)

To compare a salary with a commute, everything has to be put on the same 0 to 1 scale. Kairos does that using fixed reference points: a worst case and a best case for each objective, decided before the options are scored. Here, salary runs from 100 (worst) to 300 (best), and commute from 90 minutes (worst) to 0 (best).

Example: Offer A (startup) vs Offer B (bank)
ObjectiveWeightOffer AOffer B
Salary30%170 → 0.35150 → 0.25
Commute15%60 min → 0.3320 min → 0.78
Growth25%8/10 → 0.785/10 → 0.44
Flexibility15%6/10 → 0.564/10 → 0.33
Culture15%5/10 → 0.448/10 → 0.78
Weighted total0.500.47

Each score is (value − worst) ÷ (best − worst). Multiply by the weight, add them up, and Offer A leads, but only by 0.03. That's close, and Kairos knows it.

Why fixed reference points? Many scoring methods rescale against the best and worst options in the current list. Add a third option and the scale shifts, so A and B can swap places even though nothing about A or B changed. That's called rank reversal. With fixed reference points, adding or removing an option never changes how the others compare. I chose this over TOPSIS and AHP after reading the literature on both.

Step 5b: Monte Carlo simulation

Real numbers aren't exact. A salary offer might shift a little. A commute changes every day. So instead of testing one fixed set of values, Kairos runs the comparison thousands of times, nudging the uncertain values by a realistic margin each time (about 5% for a quoted salary, about 15% for a commute estimate).

Example result

Across 10,000 runs, Offer A came out ahead in about 91%. So A is the likely winner, but not a certainty, and the report says exactly that instead of declaring a flat winner.

There's a trap here that I fixed. If a domain has no volatility set, the simulation quietly does nothing and every run is identical, which looks like stability but is really a missing input. Kairos detects that case and tells the report not to present it as evidence.

Step 5c: sensitivity analysis

Next question: what would change the answer? Kairos moves each importance weight up and down and finds the point where the winner flips.

Example result

Offer A wins as long as commute stays below about 21% of your priorities. You gave it 15%. If commute matters more to you than that, Offer B takes over. The same happens if culture goes above about 23%. So this decision rests on how much you care about commute and culture, and that's far more useful to know than "A wins".

Step 5d: stability of your priorities (SMAA-O)

People are rarely sure of exact percentages. They know "salary matters most, then growth". SMAA-O handles that. It keeps your order of importance but tries thousands of different weight combinations that fit that order, and counts how often each option wins.

Example result

Keeping salary first and growth second, Offer A wins under about 94% of reasonable weightings. Strong, but not total.

If this check disagrees with the main score (say, the main score picks A but most weightings pick B), the report is not allowed to give a confident recommendation and has to name both options. I added that rule after a live test showed the report recommending a winner that the stability check didn't support.

Step 6: the report

All of the above goes into a plain-language report. The LLM writes it, but it's boxed in:

  • Every figure it quotes is checked against the computed results. Anything that isn't found is flagged instead of shown.
  • It can't call a close result a clear win.
  • It has to say what could change the outcome, using the real percentages from the analysis.
Example: how the example would read

"Offer A comes out slightly ahead, and it wins in about 91% of simulated outcomes. But this is close. If commute matters more to you than roughly 21% of your decision, Offer B is the better choice. The biggest unknowns are your own ratings for culture and growth, so those are worth a second look before you decide."

Design decisions

Never invent a number

If something has no real measure, Kairos says so and asks you to rate it. A made-up figure would make the whole answer look more precise than it is, which is the exact failure Kairos exists to avoid.

One engine for every domain

I didn't want a car calculator and a job calculator. Engine A has no domain-specific logic. A domain is a thin configuration that lists objectives and units. I tested it on two unrelated decisions, buying a car and comparing job offers, and the same engine handled both.

Honest about uncertainty

Close results are called close. Missing data is called missing. The report is built to inform a decision, not to pretend the decision has already been made.

Decide in steps, and keep a record

Every analysis is saved as a snapshot, so you can come back to a decision, see what the numbers said at the time, and pick up where you left off.

What's next

Web research. You shouldn't have to type in every data point. Ask about iPhone 17 Pro vs Galaxy S26 Ultra vs Pixel 10 Pro and Kairos should research the specs itself. But it can't trust online numbers blindly. Each value has to stay tied to its objective and be reviewable before it is used. That raises a harder question: how does Kairos decide whether something is even measurable? Finding a number isn't the same as finding the right one.

Engine B. Engine A handles what numbers can model. Engine B is for the decisions a spreadsheet can't hold: career direction, relationships, values. It will use real frameworks instead of vibes, and it will be honest that some things can only be informed, never predicted. It isn't started, and I won't pretend otherwise.
